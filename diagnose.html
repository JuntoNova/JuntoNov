Single-layer analysis fails

Most cost-of-living work studies one layer in isolation: the politics, or the statute, or the polling, or the market. Plans built that way stall the moment any of the other three push back.

These four layers are interdependent. Public and political support define the band of policy that can stick. Policy sets the rules that shape prices. The market shows what households and capital are already doing. A diagnosis that maps all four is the only way to see which levers are real and which are theater.

What we map

01 · PUBLIC OPINION

The band of support

Polling and focus groups that measure what people will accept, reject, and fight for - including intensity, not just direction.

02 · POLITICAL INCENTIVES

What can survive an election

Whip counts and incentive maps: who gains, who loses, and which elected officials will carry reform past the next cycle.

03 · POLICY REALITY

Where the rules sit

Where current law and regulation sit on the continuum between rules that raise costs and rules that lower them.

04 · MARKET / SUPPLY

What is already happening

Spending trends, revealed preferences, existing proof points, and where capital is flowing or blocked.

Why the full continuum matters

In education, pure cost-lowering policy sat outside the public's support band. The status quo sat far to the cost-raising end of what people would accept. The workable path - universal school choice and low-cost private options - only became visible once expressed preferences, revealed preferences, and political incentives were read on the same continuum.

That is the difference between a plan that stalls and a plan that lowers costs and expands ownership.
