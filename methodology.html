Methodology

Every dot gets two scores: how much money families stand to gain, and how likely the change is to happen. Here is the math.

Up: money at stake for families (0 to 100)

What families would gain if the change happens: lower costs, higher income or more assets. For a value, money at stake comes from the rule the value supports. For a proposal or a practical change, three parts, added up:

  • Dollars, up to 50. Money per affected household per year. $10 scores 5, $100 scores 20, $1,000 scores 35, $10,000 or more scores 50. Each tenfold rise adds 15 points, so big numbers don't drown out the rest.
  • Reach, up to 30. The share of households in that city, state or country that feel the change. Everyone is 30. One in ten is 3.
  • Ownership, up to 20. 20 if the change directly raises income or assets. 10 if the change mainly lowers the price of something you'll own, like a home. 0 if the change only lowers a bill.

Each dot has one dollar figure, rounded to three digits, and the card shows how we got the figure: every input, where the input comes from, and the math.

One-time costs are spread over 30 years for a home and about 10 years for fees and training. We never add figures that overlap.

Right: how likely the change is to happen (0 to 100)

Cultural: a value to stand for. The share who agree in a public poll.

Structural: a rule to change. Every proposal starts at 50, then moves by who has to act, hardest first: a constitutional amendment minus 45, an act of Congress minus 25, a federal agency rule minus 10, an executive order minus 5, a state legislature or a statewide ballot vote plus 5, a state agency rule plus 10, a local ballot vote plus 15, a city or county council plus 20. So, other things equal, a local change scores more likely than a state change, and a state change more likely than a federal one. Done somewhere already plus 15. Poll support of 55 percent or more plus 15, under 45 percent minus 15. Strong organized opposition minus 15, moderate minus 5. Extra hurdles like 60 Senate votes or a supermajority minus 10.

Practical: a change that moves the money now. Set by where the rules already allow the change and whether people already make the change there.

Two confidence ratings

Bottom line. Rates the dollar evidence only. High when one source states the dollar figure. Medium when the math combines published numbers. Low when at least one input has no published number, or no figure exists. The card names that input. A value takes the rating of the action the value backs.

Likely to happen. Rates the feasibility evidence only. For a value: High with an independent poll, Medium when an interested group paid for the poll or the poll asked a nearby question. For a proposal or a practical change: High when the change already happened somewhere and an independent poll measured support, Medium with one of those or with a precedent plus a sponsored poll, Low otherwise.
